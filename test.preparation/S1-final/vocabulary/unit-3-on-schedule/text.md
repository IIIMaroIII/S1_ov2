TOPIC 3 — ON SCHEDULE (≤2 minutes)

When a project slips, it leaves a bitter taste in our mouths, because delays usually reveal deeper issues. In reality, everyone can have a part in its downfall—by ignoring risks, under-communicating, or assuming someone else owns the problem. A classic pattern is a long period of growing expectations combined with a lack of centralized leadership. Then you start to experience high executive turnover in the period, priorities keep shifting, and the schedule becomes unstable.

In practice, projects stay on track when the team communicates early and often. Someone asks for a brief update, and if it becomes clear that things aren’t running as smoothly as expected, they clarify the problem to make sure everyone is aligned. Then they suggest a concrete next step, and if a proposal doesn’t seem convincing, they challenge it politely and explain why—because avoiding disagreement rarely prevents delays; it usually lets issues grow.

Sometimes the project was postponed due to budget constraints, and expenses can easily go out of control without supervision. Other times, we’ve hit a problem with the current approach, and the team ran into problems during the implementation phase. That’s how the construction project went over budget—small issues compounding into big overruns.

To prevent that, teams need to step up their game: by rehearsing, checking assumptions, and catching errors early. Doing a dry run before a presentation or product launch helps prevent last-minute surprises. If you skip that, dust can collect in every nook and cranny—meaning small hidden issues quietly build up until they cause delays. When the team is aligned, a bit of urgency can be helpful: people get revved up before a key milestone, and with the right focus, the new campaign can really hit a home run.
